The Iran war is not over. The Gulf is already paying the price.

NavvyaSignal Strategic Outlook 2027–2030 | Part 3 of 8 | West Asia · Energy & Maritime · Global Politics | Updated 19 November 2026

The bottom line: The confrontation involving Iran, Israel and the United States remains unresolved. A failed diplomatic framework, renewed US naval blockade and continuing attacks around the Strait of Hormuz have made shipping and energy security a live economic problem, not a hypothetical risk. The central question for 2027 is whether the region can restore predictable trade before prolonged disruption undermines investment, food costs and economic diversification.

An unfinished war, not an aftermath

The current crisis has deep roots. The US- and UK-backed overthrow of Iranian prime minister Mohammad Mossadegh in 1953, the 1979 revolution and US embassy hostage crisis, and the 1980–88 Iran–Iraq war all shaped the region's security relationships. The 2015 nuclear agreement showed that negotiated limits were possible. The US withdrawal in 2018 and Iran's subsequent departures from the agreement exposed how vulnerable that framework was to political reversal.

The Congressional Research Service’s September 2026 chronology dates the new US and Israeli strikes to 28 February 2026. An April ceasefire and June memorandum failed to produce a durable settlement. The Joint Maritime Information Center’s 18 November advisory dates the US blockade of Iranian ports to 14 July 2026.

This matters because markets and governments cannot plan around a conflict that repeatedly appears to ease and then intensifies.

Hormuz: open to some vessels, safe for none to assume

The Strait of Hormuz is a critical passage for oil and liquefied natural gas. The US Energy Information Administration estimates that roughly 20 million barrels a day of oil passed through it in 2024—about one-fifth of global petroleum liquids consumption. [3]

The Joint Maritime Information Center’s 18 November advisory rated the regional maritime threat severe and noted continued risks around the strait. Transit conditions remain uncertain.

Different monitoring organisations count different categories of ships. Their figures should not be combined to manufacture a single percentage fall in traffic. The reliable conclusion is narrower and more important: passage is possible, but commercial risk and uncertainty remain elevated.

Pipelines, naval escorts, alternative routes and inventories can absorb some disruption. None eliminates the cost of operating around a contested chokepoint.

The Gulf's economic paradox

Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar face different exposures, but the same dilemma. Higher energy prices can support revenue for some producers while interrupted exports, insurance premiums, financing costs and delayed projects damage their broader economies. Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 and the UAE's investments in aviation, ports, finance, tourism and technology depend on the reliable movement of capital, people and goods.

The IMF’s October 2026 Middle East and Central Asia Regional Economic Outlook (Table 1.2) projected real GDP growth of −3.9% in 2026 and +7.7% in 2027 for the UAE, −3.3% and +7.9% for Saudi Arabia, and −15.6% and +18.8% for Qatar. These are forecasts, not measured outcomes. Crucially, the IMF's baseline assumes that reopening of Hormuz resumes in March 2027 and that conditions broadly return to pre-war levels by October 2027. If that path slips, the projections may not hold. [7]

Qatar illustrates the exposure of gas markets. The EIA reported that Qatar exported an average 10.6 billion cubic feet a day of LNG in 2025, with more than 80% going to Asian buyers. In July 2026, the agency described falling Qatari exports amid the Hormuz disruption. That account establishes a direction of pressure, not a verified full-year 2026 export volume. [8]

Diversification is not the same as resilience. Building more non-oil industries helps only if the trade, energy and financial systems supporting them remain dependable.

Why the consequences reach India's farms

Natural gas is a key feedstock and energy source for ammonia, the foundation of many nitrogen fertilisers. A gas shock can therefore raise fertiliser costs and eventually pressure farm budgets, subsidy bills and food prices. The transmission is neither immediate nor inevitable: inventories, alternative suppliers and government intervention can cushion it.

India illustrates the exposure. The IEA identifies ammonia as the starting point for mineral nitrogen fertilisers. Disruptions to gas or fertiliser supply can put pressure on import costs, depending on inventory, alternative suppliers and policy responses.

The broader point does not depend on those exact percentages. India and other large importers face real exposure when Gulf gas, fertiliser and shipping markets are disrupted.

Two chokepoints, one global supply chain

Hormuz is not the region's only maritime vulnerability. The Red Sea–Bab el-Mandeb–Suez route links Asian production to European markets. Attacks and rerouting have already shown how quickly shipping firms can be pushed toward longer voyages around the Cape of Good Hope. Longer journeys use more fuel, ships and working capital, and make delivery schedules less predictable.

The conflicts affecting these corridors have different causes and actors; they should not be collapsed into a single war. Their economic effects, however, can reinforce one another. An energy shock in the Gulf alongside freight disruption in the Red Sea is more damaging than either problem in isolation.

A regional balance without easy alliances

Saudi Arabia needs investment and energy security. The UAE needs commercial openness and reliable international connections. Iran faces sanctions, security pressures and the consequences of continuing conflict. Israel prioritises deterrence and security. Turkey pursues its own regional and economic interests. None can assume that the preferences of outside powers will always match its own.

The restoration of Saudi–Iran diplomatic relations in 2023 and the UAE's diplomatic relations with Israel under the 2020 Abraham Accords demonstrate that regional governments can keep multiple channels open even amid rivalry. They do not erase the disputes that made those channels necessary. [11]

The United States remains a major security actor; China, Europe and Russia matter through different combinations of trade, investment, energy and diplomacy. Gulf governments will continue to seek room to manoeuvre rather than accept a single, rigid alignment.

For the UAE and India, this creates a shared interest in resilient trade and energy links. Their Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement entered into force in 2022. The Embassy of India in Abu Dhabi's published community estimate—3.5 million Indian nationals in 2021—is dated and should not be mistaken for a current population count. [12]

Three plausible paths for 2027

Managed de-escalation. A credible, enforceable US–Iran arrangement reduces attacks, lifts the blockade and allows maritime traffic and insurance conditions to recover. This is the path closest to the IMF's baseline assumption.

Prolonged contested passage. Diplomacy continues without a durable settlement. Some shipping moves, but recurring incidents, restrictions and higher costs make the Gulf an unreliable corridor. This extends the conditions seen in late 2026; it is a scenario, not a certainty.

Wider escalation. New strikes on energy infrastructure or commercial vessels cause a sharper supply shock, increasing the pressure on governments, businesses and households well beyond the Middle East.

The strongest signals to watch are not headlines alone: changes in the blockade, attacks on vessels, comparable shipping-transit data, LNG loadings, freight and insurance costs, and evidence of a binding diplomatic agreement.

The outlook

NavvyaSignal's assessment: Without a durable and enforceable agreement, Gulf shipping and energy markets are likely to remain vulnerable into early 2027. That is a conditional editorial judgment, not a numerical forecast. Escorts, alternative routes, inventories or a faster diplomatic breakthrough could shorten the disruption; further strikes could make it substantially worse.

The region's economic ambitions remain substantial. But the lesson of this crisis is that diversification cannot succeed on investment announcements alone. It also requires trade routes that function, institutions that can manage conflict, and a security environment in which businesses can plan beyond the next incident.

The defining question for 2027 is not simply how this war ends. It is whether the Middle East can build an economic order resilient enough to survive the next confrontation.
