NAVVYASIGNAL · THE WORLD AHEAD 2027 · PART 5 OF 8

How artificial intelligence, semiconductors, cyber capabilities and autonomous weapons are changing the balance of global power—and why technological superiority may no longer guarantee strategic dominance.

Tech & AI / Global Politics / Defence & Security · November 19, 2026

The next struggle for global power may be decided as much in data centres and semiconductor factories as on conventional battlefields.

For centuries, military and economic superiority depended heavily on control of physical resources: land, industrial production, energy, shipping routes and armed forces.

Those advantages remain important. But a new layer of strategic competition has emerged.

Artificial intelligence is becoming embedded in economic production, intelligence analysis, military planning, cybersecurity, scientific research and critical infrastructure.

Semiconductors underpin the computing systems on which these capabilities depend. Satellites connect military and civilian networks. Autonomous systems are changing the economics of surveillance and warfare.

The result is a fundamental shift in the meaning of national power.

A country may possess a large military but lack access to advanced computing. Another may dominate specialised chipmaking without possessing a comparable conventional military force. A smaller state may use inexpensive drones and software to impose significant costs on a much larger opponent.

These developments challenge the assumption that technological superiority translates automatically into strategic control.

They also create new dependencies.

Advanced AI requires electricity, computing infrastructure, specialised hardware, data, communications networks and skilled personnel. Disruption to any of these can weaken the systems built upon them.

The Economist's World Ahead 2027 identifies artificial intelligence, Taiwan and commercial space as important themes. Together, they point towards a broader transformation: technology is becoming inseparable from geopolitical competition.

The defining question for 2027 is not simply which country develops the most powerful AI system. It is which countries can translate technological capabilities into sustainable economic, military and institutional advantages without creating unacceptable vulnerabilities.

I. From industrial warfare to algorithmic power

The relationship between technology and military power is not new.

The Industrial Revolution transformed warfare by expanding the production of weapons, railways, ships and communications equipment.

During the First World War, industrial capacity became essential to sustaining large-scale military operations.

The Second World War demonstrated the strategic importance of radar, cryptography, aviation, industrial logistics and nuclear research.

The Cold War extended technological competition into missiles, satellites, computing and global communications.

The emergence of the internet introduced new vulnerabilities and capabilities involving information networks.

Artificial intelligence represents another stage in this historical progression.

Its significance lies in the potential to accelerate tasks previously dependent on human analysis, judgement and coordination.

Five historical turning points

Period Technological transformation Strategic consequence
19th century Industrial manufacturing and railways Mass production and military mobilisation
1914–1945 Aviation, mechanisation, radar and cryptography Industrial and information-intensive warfare
1945–1991 Nuclear weapons, satellites and computers Deterrence and global strategic competition
1990–2020 Internet, GPS, precision weapons and cyber operations Networked military and economic systems
2020s onward AI, autonomous systems and advanced computing Faster analysis, automation and new vulnerabilities

The current transition differs from earlier ones because many important technologies are developed commercially before being adapted for national-security applications.

This complicates the relationship between governments and private companies.

A technology firm may control infrastructure essential to national security without operating as a traditional defence contractor.

A commercial satellite network may become strategically important during a conflict.

An AI model developed for civilian applications may also have military or intelligence uses.

NavvyaSignal assessment: The boundary between civilian technological infrastructure and national-security capability is becoming increasingly difficult to maintain.

II. The new military equation: Cheap systems against expensive platforms

One of the most important lessons of contemporary warfare is that the cost of an attack can be dramatically lower than the cost of defending against it.

A relatively inexpensive drone may threaten a vehicle, radar installation or other high-value asset. Defending against large numbers of such systems can require costly sensors, electronic countermeasures, interceptors and trained personnel.

This does not make conventional military platforms obsolete.

Tanks, aircraft, artillery, naval vessels and infantry remain essential in many military operations.

But their effectiveness increasingly depends on how well they are integrated with surveillance, communications, electronic warfare and autonomous systems.

The changing economics of defence

The relevant comparison is not simply the price of a drone against the price of a missile.

It is the total cost of producing, deploying, detecting, intercepting and replacing the systems involved.

NATO's response

On 5 November 2026, NATO’s procurement agency announced that it had selected 16 counter-drone solutions from five companies for its new marketplace. A procurement announcement establishes the selections and their intended use; it does not establish outcomes in combat.

The initiative aims to accelerate access to systems capable of protecting infrastructure, military installations, borders and mobile forces.

The announcement illustrates a wider institutional shift.

Military organisations are increasingly concerned not only with acquiring sophisticated weapons but also with adapting procurement systems to rapidly changing technologies.

NavvyaSignal assessment: The next generation of military advantage will depend on the ability to combine affordable systems, rapid production, reliable intelligence and effective countermeasures. The most expensive platform will not necessarily provide the most economical defence.

III. Artificial intelligence and the speed of military decision-making

AI's military significance extends well beyond autonomous weapons.

Machine-learning systems can assist with analysing imagery, detecting patterns, prioritising information, identifying maintenance requirements and supporting logistics.

In intelligence operations, the volume of available information can exceed what human analysts can process efficiently.

Satellites, drones, communications systems and other sensors generate large quantities of data.

AI can help identify potentially relevant information more quickly.

But faster processing does not guarantee accurate interpretation.

The problem of algorithmic confidence

AI systems can make mistakes.

They may misclassify objects, rely on incomplete information, perform poorly under unfamiliar conditions or produce outputs that appear convincing despite being incorrect.

These limitations are particularly consequential in military environments.

An erroneous classification in a commercial recommendation system may inconvenience a customer.

An erroneous classification in a military targeting process could contribute to unlawful or unintended harm.

The distinction between decision support and autonomous decision-making is therefore essential.

A system that helps a human analyst examine evidence presents different risks from one authorised to select and engage targets without meaningful human intervention.

The escalation problem

Speed can create strategic advantages, but it can also reduce the time available for verification and diplomacy.

If competing military systems interpret one another's actions through automated processes, errors may propagate before human decision-makers fully understand the situation.

The concern is not that AI systems inevitably initiate wars.

It is that poorly governed automation could increase the likelihood or speed of escalation.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Military AI should be evaluated by reliability, accountability and operational effectiveness—not simply by how quickly it produces a recommendation.

IV. Semiconductors: The strategic resource behind modern computing

dresden-chip-academy.de

Artificial intelligence depends on physical infrastructure.

Training and operating advanced models require computing hardware, memory, networking equipment, electricity and cooling systems.

At the centre of this infrastructure are semiconductors.

The semiconductor supply chain is geographically distributed and technically specialised.

Chip design, manufacturing equipment, fabrication, packaging and materials production involve companies across several countries.

This creates efficiency through specialisation.

It also creates strategic dependencies.

Why Taiwan matters

Taiwan plays an exceptionally important role in advanced semiconductor manufacturing.

Its position connects technological competition with the security situation in the Taiwan Strait.

A severe disruption could affect industries far beyond consumer electronics.

Advanced chips support data centres, telecommunications, industrial systems, scientific computing and many defence applications.

However, the semiconductor industry is not located entirely in Taiwan.

The United States, South Korea, Japan, European countries and China all contribute different capabilities to the global supply chain.

The strategic vulnerability lies partly in the concentration of particular advanced manufacturing capabilities.

The US-China technology competition

The United States has used export restrictions to limit Chinese access to certain advanced computing technologies and semiconductor-manufacturing capabilities.

China has pursued domestic technological development to reduce reliance on foreign suppliers.

These efforts involve more than commercial rivalry.

They reflect the growing perception that access to advanced computing can influence military modernisation, intelligence capabilities and industrial competitiveness.

The US–China Economic and Security Review Commission’s November 2026 report describes Chinese efforts to integrate AI into military command and logistics. The report describes development and adoption efforts; it does not demonstrate operational effectiveness.

The limitations of technological restrictions

Export controls can slow access to particular technologies, but their effectiveness depends on enforcement, international cooperation and the availability of alternatives.

Restrictions can also encourage domestic substitution and technological innovation.

The long-term consequences are therefore difficult to predict.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Semiconductor policy has become a form of strategic statecraft. But technological independence is expensive, and complete national self-sufficiency across the entire semiconductor supply chain is unrealistic for most countries.

V. Cybersecurity: When civilian infrastructure becomes a strategic target

Cyber operations represent another area where economic and military security overlap.

Modern societies depend on interconnected digital systems for electricity, banking, communications, transportation, healthcare and government administration.

Disruption to these systems can create consequences extending well beyond the immediate technical incident.

AI introduces both defensive opportunities and additional risks.

AI-assisted cyber defence

Machine-learning systems can help identify suspicious activity, detect anomalies and prioritise security alerts.

They may reduce the burden on human analysts and improve the speed of incident response.

However, automated detection systems can generate false positives or overlook unfamiliar attack patterns.

Human oversight and robust system design remain important.

AI-enabled offensive capabilities

AI can also assist malicious actors in generating deceptive communications, analysing software and scaling certain elements of cyber operations.

The Economist's 2027 outlook explicitly warns that more capable AI systems could contribute to increasingly serious cyber threats.

The Economist Group

The precise scale of that effect remains uncertain.

Cybersecurity outcomes depend on access controls, software quality, organisational practices, network architecture and the capabilities of defenders.

The geopolitical challenge

Attributing a cyberattack to a particular government can be difficult.

Technical indicators may suggest involvement, but public evidence does not always establish responsibility conclusively.

This creates diplomatic complications.

A serious cyber incident could be interpreted as criminal activity, espionage, coercion or an act associated with military conflict.

Different interpretations can produce very different responses.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Cyber resilience is becoming a component of national economic security. Protecting digital infrastructure is increasingly as important as defending physical transport and energy networks.

VI. Space: The infrastructure above the battlefield

jll.com

Space systems have become essential to both civilian and military activity.

Satellites support navigation, weather forecasting, communications, environmental monitoring and intelligence gathering.

The Economist expects cheaper launches and further development of commercial space infrastructure during 2027.

These developments could expand access to orbital services while creating additional security challenges.

From national programmes to commercial networks

During the Cold War, space activity was dominated by governments.

Commercial companies now play major roles in satellite manufacturing, launch services and communications.

This shift creates new possibilities for innovation and cost reduction.

But it also raises questions about the responsibilities of private companies whose infrastructure becomes strategically important.

A commercial satellite service may support civilian connectivity while also being used during military operations.

Space and strategic vulnerability

Satellites can be affected by electronic interference, cyber operations, physical threats and orbital debris.

A disruption to satellite services can influence navigation, communications and other systems on Earth.

Consequently, governments must consider how to maintain essential services when space infrastructure becomes unavailable or degraded.

Orbital data centres: Promise versus feasibility

The idea of placing computing infrastructure in orbit has attracted attention.

Potential advantages might include access to solar energy and particular orbital operating conditions.

But substantial challenges remain.

These include launch costs, radiation exposure, cooling, maintenance, communications latency and equipment replacement.

The commercial viability of orbital data centres is therefore not established simply because launch costs decline.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Space will become increasingly important to economic and military resilience, but the most valuable near-term applications may remain communications, navigation and observation rather than more speculative computing projects.

VII. The emerging technological power map

Technological competition is often described as a contest between the United States and China.

That framing captures an important rivalry but overlooks the specialised capabilities of other economies.

Country or region Major strategic strengths Principal vulnerabilities
United States AI research, leading chip design, cloud infrastructure, venture capital and military technology Manufacturing dependencies, infrastructure costs and supply-chain exposure
China Industrial scale, manufacturing ecosystems, domestic market and technological investment Restrictions on certain advanced technologies and external market exposure
Taiwan Advanced semiconductor fabrication Geopolitical concentration and security exposure
European Union Semiconductor equipment, industrial technology, research and regulation Fragmented markets, scaling challenges and defence coordination
Japan Materials, precision manufacturing and semiconductor equipment Demographic and economic constraints
South Korea Memory chips, electronics and advanced manufacturing Export dependence and regional security risks
India Software services, engineering talent, digital infrastructure and growing technology markets Advanced chipmaking capacity, research depth and infrastructure gaps
UAE and Gulf states Capital, energy resources, infrastructure investment and international connectivity Dependence on imported advanced technology and specialist talent

These are broad structural assessments rather than numerical rankings.

The important point is that no single country controls every component of the technological ecosystem.

The United States: Innovation and platform power

American companies occupy influential positions in advanced AI development, cloud services and semiconductor design.

The country's research institutions, capital markets and entrepreneurial ecosystem provide substantial advantages.

But leadership in software does not eliminate dependencies on international manufacturing and specialised equipment.

China: Scale and technological substitution

China's strengths include manufacturing capacity, industrial coordination and a large domestic market.

Its efforts to develop advanced domestic technologies reflect both economic ambition and concern about external restrictions.

The key uncertainty is how quickly domestic alternatives can close gaps in particularly demanding technologies.

Europe: Specialisation without complete strategic control

Europe possesses significant industrial and technological capabilities, including specialised semiconductor equipment and advanced engineering.

Its challenge is to convert those strengths into globally competitive technology businesses and coherent strategic policy.

India: The opportunity to move beyond services

India's software expertise and growing digital economy create opportunities in AI applications, cybersecurity, engineering and technology services.

But the country faces difficult choices about where to invest in expensive physical infrastructure.

Attempting to reproduce every part of the global semiconductor supply chain would require enormous resources.

A more effective approach may involve targeted capabilities, strategic partnerships and the development of commercially competitive technology sectors.

The UAE: From technology adoption to technological capability

The UAE is investing in AI infrastructure, research, digital government and advanced technology partnerships.

Its advantages include access to capital, energy infrastructure and international business networks.

However, purchasing advanced technology is not the same as possessing independent technological capability.

Long-term competitiveness requires research capacity, specialist talent, secure infrastructure and the ability to develop and commercialise intellectual property.

NavvyaSignal assessment: The next phase of technological competition will reward countries that build sustainable capabilities rather than relying exclusively on technology imports or headline investment announcements.

VIII. The economic paradox: AI could increase productivity while concentrating power

AI offers potentially significant economic benefits.

Businesses may automate repetitive tasks, improve analysis, accelerate software development and enhance customer services.

Researchers may use AI to support scientific discovery and data interpretation.

Public institutions may improve administrative efficiency.

But these benefits will not necessarily be distributed evenly.

The concentration problem

Advanced AI infrastructure requires substantial capital.

This can favour large technology companies and well-financed institutions.

If access to computing resources, specialised hardware and proprietary systems becomes concentrated, smaller companies and countries may face disadvantages.

At the same time, open models and falling inference costs could reduce some barriers to adoption.

The future structure of the AI industry remains contested.

Employment and organisational change

AI may automate particular tasks without eliminating every occupation associated with them.

A profession can change significantly even when the number of people employed in it does not immediately decline.

New tasks and industries may emerge, while others become less valuable.

The effects will depend on the pace of adoption, complementary investment, regulation and workforce adaptation.

The Economist's official outlook argues that some concerns about AI-related job losses and environmental consequences are overstated.

The Economist Group

That is an editorial judgement, not a settled empirical conclusion.

The governance challenge

Governments face a difficult balance.

Excessively restrictive regulation may slow useful innovation.

Insufficient oversight may increase risks involving privacy, discrimination, security and accountability.

The challenge is to regulate applications according to their consequences rather than treating every use of AI as equally dangerous.

NavvyaSignal assessment: The long-term economic impact of AI will depend less on model demonstrations than on measurable productivity, competitive markets, institutional adaptation and broad access to useful capabilities.

IX. The danger of automated escalation

One of the most serious concerns surrounding military AI is the possibility that automated systems could accelerate confrontation.

This risk can arise through several mechanisms.

An intelligence system may misinterpret activity. A decision-support system may present uncertain information with excessive confidence. A military organisation may become overly dependent on automated recommendations.

Competing states may also fear that delaying a response would leave them at a disadvantage.

The combination of uncertainty and compressed decision time can create dangerous incentives.

Why human control matters

Meaningful human involvement is not merely a procedural requirement.

It provides opportunities to consider context, question unreliable information and evaluate political consequences.

However, human oversight must be effective.

A person who routinely approves automated recommendations without sufficient time or information may provide little meaningful control.

Accountability therefore requires more than placing a human signature at the end of a technical process.

It requires systems designed to support informed decisions.

International governance

The challenge is complicated by the absence of a universally accepted and enforceable framework governing all military uses of AI.

States differ in their assessments of acceptable autonomy, military necessity and verification.

The International Committee of the Red Cross explains that existing international humanitarian law applies to the use of autonomous weapons in armed conflict.

But practical questions about accountability, testing and operational deployment remain difficult.

NavvyaSignal assessment: The most dangerous technological competition is one in which states accelerate deployment because they fear falling behind, while reducing the time available to establish adequate safeguards.

X. Three technological scenarios for 2027

These scenarios are independent NavvyaSignal analytical frameworks, not probability-weighted forecasts.

Scenario A — Competitive but governed innovation

Lower-risk pathway

AI capabilities improve, commercial adoption expands and governments strengthen safeguards around high-risk applications.

Defence organisations integrate new technologies while maintaining operational accountability.

Potential outcome: Productivity gains, improved defensive capabilities and manageable technological competition.

Scenario B — Accelerating technological fragmentation

Planning scenario

Export restrictions, national-security concerns and industrial policies encourage increasingly separate technology ecosystems.

AI deployment expands, but international cooperation becomes more difficult.

Potential outcome: Higher costs, duplicated infrastructure, uneven access and intensified strategic competition.

Scenario C — Technology-driven security crisis

Severe downside

A serious cyber incident, military automation failure or confrontation involving critical technology infrastructure triggers broader geopolitical escalation.

Potential outcome: Disruption to communications, finance, supply chains and military stability.

The scenarios are not mutually exclusive across all sectors or regions. Different countries could experience different conditions simultaneously.

XII. The strategic question: Who actually controls technological power?

The competition for technological leadership is often reduced to rankings of AI models, semiconductor performance or defence expenditure.

These measures matter, but they provide an incomplete picture.

A country can develop advanced AI systems while depending on foreign manufacturing.

It can possess sophisticated weapons while lacking the industrial capacity to replace them quickly.

It can invest heavily in data centres while struggling to generate adequate commercial returns.

And it can accumulate technological capabilities without establishing the institutions necessary to use them responsibly.

The deeper structure of technological power consists of five interdependent elements:

  1. Capability: The ability to develop and deploy effective technologies.
  2. Infrastructure: Reliable access to computing, energy, communications and manufacturing.
  3. Economic sustainability: The capacity to finance innovation and generate productive returns.
  4. Resilience: The ability to continue operating during disruption.
  5. Governance: Institutions capable of managing risks, accountability and strategic consequences.

Weakness in one element can undermine strength in the others.

Technological leadership is therefore not a single achievement.

It is a continuously maintained system of industrial, scientific, economic and institutional capabilities.

Conclusion: The future belongs to those who can govern the technology they build

Artificial intelligence is changing the relationship between economic productivity, military capability and geopolitical influence.

Autonomous systems are altering the economics of warfare. Semiconductors have become strategic assets. Cybersecurity is central to national resilience. Commercial space infrastructure increasingly supports essential services.

These developments create opportunities for innovation, growth and improved security.

They also introduce vulnerabilities that governments and businesses are still learning to manage.

The United States and China will remain central to this competition, but they will not determine every outcome.

Taiwan, Europe, Japan, South Korea, India and the Gulf states possess capabilities and relationships that influence the wider technological system.

The strategic challenge is not simply to acquire the most advanced tools.

It is to integrate them into resilient economies, accountable institutions and credible security arrangements.

The defining technological struggle of 2027 may not be between humans and machines. It may be between institutions capable of controlling powerful technologies and institutions that deploy them faster than they can understand their consequences.

That distinction will matter long after the current generation of AI models, drones and computing systems has been replaced.

The countries that succeed will not necessarily be those that automate the most decisions.

They will be those that retain the ability to make the right decisions when automation fails.
