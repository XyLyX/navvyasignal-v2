As global power shifts, can India and the Gulf states turn geopolitical uncertainty into economic advantage—or will their dependence on energy, trade, technology and international capital expose them to a new era of vulnerability?

West Asia / India / UAE / Global Economy · November 19, 2026

evardo.com

The next global economic order may be shaped not only by the world's largest powers, but by the countries connecting them.

For much of the twentieth century, international power was understood through relatively clear divisions.

The Cold War placed the United States and the Soviet Union at the centre of global competition. After the Soviet collapse, American military and financial influence became dominant.

China's economic rise subsequently challenged that concentration of power.

But the international system approaching 2027 is more complicated than a contest between Washington and Beijing.

India is seeking a larger role in global manufacturing, technology and diplomacy. The United Arab Emirates has established itself as an international centre for commerce, logistics, finance and investment. Saudi Arabia is pursuing a far-reaching economic transformation. Qatar remains strategically important to global natural gas markets.

These countries are not equivalent in size, resources or political influence.

Yet they share an important strategic characteristic: their economic interests cross geopolitical boundaries.

They maintain relationships with Western economies while engaging China and other major powers. They depend on international trade but seek greater control over critical supply chains. They welcome foreign investment while developing domestic industrial and technological capabilities.

This creates opportunities that did not exist in the same form during the Cold War.

It also creates vulnerabilities.

A military escalation in the Gulf can affect Indian energy imports. Financial instability in the United States can influence borrowing costs in Dubai. Restrictions on advanced semiconductors can affect technology ambitions in Abu Dhabi and Bengaluru.

The defining question for India and the Gulf in 2027 is whether they can preserve strategic flexibility while building the domestic capabilities needed to withstand a more fragmented international system.

I. From non-alignment to strategic autonomy

India's contemporary foreign policy has historical roots in the Non-Aligned Movement.

During the Cold War, newly independent countries sought varying degrees of freedom from the competing American and Soviet blocs.

India's first prime minister, Jawaharlal Nehru, was among the movement's leading figures.

Non-alignment did not mean political neutrality on every international question. Nor did it prevent India from developing closer relationships with particular powers.

It represented an effort to preserve independent decision-making.

The contemporary concept of strategic autonomy builds on that tradition but operates in a different environment.

India maintains significant defence and technology relationships with the United States while preserving longstanding ties with Russia. It participates in groupings such as BRICS and the Quad, whose memberships and purposes differ.

These relationships are not necessarily contradictory.

They reflect an attempt to pursue national interests through multiple partnerships.

The Gulf's different historical trajectory

The Gulf states followed a different path.

Their modern economic development was closely connected to hydrocarbon production, international energy markets and security relationships with external powers.

The United States became an important security partner for several Gulf governments.

At the same time, Asia's industrial expansion increased demand for Gulf energy.

China, India, Japan and South Korea consequently became important economic partners.

The result is a regional structure in which security relationships and commercial relationships do not always align neatly.

Why 2027 matters

As great-power competition intensifies, countries maintaining diverse partnerships may face growing pressure to choose between competing technological, financial or security arrangements.

Strategic autonomy becomes more difficult when advanced technology, payments infrastructure, investment flows and defence systems are subject to geopolitical restrictions.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Strategic autonomy should not be measured by the number of countries with which a government maintains diplomatic relations. It should be measured by its ability to make consequential decisions without unacceptable economic or security dependence.

II. India: Scale is an advantage, but execution determines power

commercialdesignindia.com

India possesses several structural advantages.

Its large domestic market supports opportunities for manufacturing, digital services and consumer industries.

Its technology workforce has substantial experience in software, engineering and global business services.

Its geographical position connects the Indian Ocean with major international trade routes.

Its political and economic relationships span several competing centres of global influence.

But these advantages do not automatically translate into economic leadership.

The manufacturing opportunity

Geopolitical competition has encouraged businesses to reconsider concentrated manufacturing supply chains.

The phrase China plus one describes strategies in which companies maintain production in China while developing additional manufacturing capacity elsewhere.

India could benefit from this diversification.

However, attracting manufacturing investment requires more than a large labour force.

Investors consider logistics costs, electricity reliability, regulatory predictability, workforce skills, supplier networks and access to international markets.

A factory is only one component of an industrial ecosystem.

The ability to deliver products reliably, competitively and at scale determines whether investment becomes sustainable.

The technology opportunity

India has an established position in software services and digital technology.

AI creates opportunities to move into higher-value applications, enterprise systems, research and automation.

But it also creates a potential threat to existing service models.

If AI reduces the labour required for certain software and business-process tasks, Indian companies may need to develop new capabilities and revenue models.

The opportunity is therefore not simply to provide more technology workers.

It is to build products, platforms and intellectual property that capture greater economic value.

The energy constraint

India remains exposed to international energy markets.

Higher oil and gas prices can influence import expenditure, inflation and industrial costs.

Energy diversification and improved efficiency can reduce some vulnerabilities, but infrastructure investment takes time.

NavvyaSignal assessment: India's strategic opportunity is substantial, but its success will depend on productivity, infrastructure quality and institutional execution rather than demographic scale alone.

III. The UAE: From regional trading hub to global economic connector

basecareer.co

The UAE's economic position is distinctive.

Its domestic population and territory are relatively small compared with major global powers.

Yet its commercial infrastructure connects markets across Asia, Europe and Africa.

Dubai's logistics and financial ecosystems, Abu Dhabi's investment capacity and the country's international connectivity provide advantages that extend beyond its domestic market.

The geography of commercial power

The UAE benefits from its location near important maritime and aviation corridors.

Ports, airports, free zones and financial institutions facilitate the movement of goods, capital, services and people.

This model creates economic opportunities without requiring the country to dominate global manufacturing or consumer demand.

Its value lies partly in enabling transactions between other economies.

But connectivity also creates exposure.

Disruption to shipping, aviation, financial markets or regional security can affect commercial activity even when domestic demand remains stable.

The investment advantage

The UAE possesses significant investment resources and has developed relationships across multiple regions.

These can support diversification into technology, infrastructure, advanced manufacturing and other sectors.

However, investment capacity should not be confused with guaranteed commercial success.

Large capital commitments can produce weak returns if projects lack sustainable demand, technical expertise or operational discipline.

The critical question is whether investment creates durable domestic capabilities.

The technology challenge

AI infrastructure offers opportunities for the UAE to strengthen its position in digital services and advanced computing.

But computing capacity depends on access to semiconductors, electricity, cooling, specialised talent and international technology partnerships.

A country can host advanced data centres without controlling the intellectual property or supply chains supporting them.

NavvyaSignal assessment: The UAE's long-term competitive advantage will depend on combining global connectivity with greater technological, industrial and institutional depth.

IV. Saudi Arabia: Economic transformation under geopolitical pressure

aawsat.com

Saudi Arabia's economic transformation is among the most ambitious diversification programmes in the region.

Vision 2030 seeks to expand non-oil activity through tourism, logistics, manufacturing, investment and other sectors.

The strategic logic is clear.

Hydrocarbon revenues have historically supported public finances and economic development. Diversification aims to create additional sources of growth and employment.

But transformation on this scale requires capital, infrastructure, skilled labour and long-term investor confidence.

The financing challenge

Major development projects can be sensitive to construction costs, borrowing conditions and revenue expectations.

A project may be technically achievable without being economically justified at its original scale or timetable.

Changing market conditions may therefore require prioritisation, redesign or delays.

These adjustments are not necessarily evidence of failure.

They can represent necessary financial discipline.

The security dimension

Saudi Arabia's economic ambitions also depend on regional stability.

Tourism, logistics and foreign investment are sensitive to perceptions of security and reliability.

The country's strategic challenge is to maintain credible defence capabilities while supporting diplomatic arrangements that reduce regional confrontation.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Saudi Arabia's transformation will ultimately be judged by the productivity and financial sustainability of its non-oil economy, not the scale of its announced projects.

V. India-UAE relations: From bilateral trade to strategic economic integration

The relationship between India and the UAE provides one of the clearest examples of how middle powers can deepen economic cooperation despite geopolitical uncertainty.

The Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) entered into force on May 1, 2022.

It established a framework for reducing trade barriers and expanding commercial cooperation.

The subsequent growth in bilateral trade has been substantial.

The trade relationship includes petroleum products, precious metals, food products, textiles, chemicals, electronics and engineering goods.

Investment cooperation extends across infrastructure, logistics, financial services, energy and technology.

Beyond trade volumes

Bilateral trade growth is important, but it is not sufficient evidence of deeper economic integration.

The more consequential indicators are whether businesses establish durable production networks, whether investment generates competitive industries, and whether regulatory arrangements reduce transaction costs.

A trading relationship dominated by the movement of commodities has different strategic characteristics from one involving advanced manufacturing, research, intellectual property and integrated supply chains.

Both countries have incentives to expand the latter.

Food security and agricultural cooperation

The UAE depends heavily on international food supply chains, while India possesses substantial agricultural production and food-processing capacity.

Cooperation could improve storage, logistics, processing and supply-chain coordination.

However, food security cannot be reduced to preferential access to one supplier.

India's own domestic food requirements, agricultural policies and climate exposure can influence export availability.

Resilience requires diversification and reliable commercial arrangements.

Energy and investment

The January 2026 bilateral engagements included a long-term LNG supply agreement involving HPCL and ADNOC Gas, as well as investment cooperation concerning the Dholera Special Investment Region.

These initiatives illustrate the relationship's expanding scope.

But announcements and agreements should be distinguished from completed investment, delivered infrastructure and realised economic returns.

NavvyaSignal assessment: India-UAE cooperation will become strategically more significant if it moves from high trade volumes towards integrated production, technology development and resilient infrastructure.

VI. The India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor: Opportunity versus execution

The India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor was launched as an initiative at the New Delhi G20 on 9 September 2023. Its proposed railway and shipping links depend on subsequent agreements, investment and implementation.

Its ambition is to improve connectivity between India, the Gulf and Europe through transport, energy and digital infrastructure.

The strategic rationale is compelling.

International trade depends on a limited number of major maritime routes. Disruption to the Suez Canal, Red Sea or other corridors can increase costs and delay shipments.

Alternative connectivity could improve resilience.

But IMEC should not be treated as an operational substitute for existing maritime routes.

What the OECD found in November 2026

The OECD’s 5 November 2026 report assessed existing infrastructure and the conditions for connecting it. India’s Ministry of External Affairs separately records a February 2024 framework agreement with the UAE and a bilateral virtual trade corridor. These are defined milestones. Neither is evidence of an operational India-to-Europe corridor.

It found that the participating economies possess important existing infrastructure, but connectivity remains uneven.

European economies have established logistics systems. Gulf countries are increasingly sophisticated transshipment hubs. India has substantial freight volumes and trade connections.

However, turning these separate strengths into an integrated corridor requires investment, regulatory coordination and operational interoperability.

The overlooked constraints

A successful corridor requires more than railway tracks and port terminals.

Customs systems must be compatible. Cargo documentation must move efficiently. Border procedures must be predictable. Infrastructure must connect physically and commercially.

Insurance, financing, security and geopolitical relationships also matter.

The proposed corridor passes through a region affected by significant political disputes.

That introduces execution risks that cannot be solved through infrastructure investment alone.

The economic test

IMEC's eventual competitiveness will depend on actual transit times, total logistics costs, reliability and the types of cargo it can transport efficiently.

A route may be strategically attractive without being commercially competitive for every shipment.

NavvyaSignal assessment: IMEC should be evaluated through measurable improvements in freight reliability and cost—not through geopolitical symbolism or announcements alone.

VII. The wider Gulf: Competition and cooperation

The Gulf Cooperation Council includes six countries with distinct economic structures and strategic priorities.

Saudi Arabia and the UAE are expanding non-oil industries and international investment.

Qatar remains an important natural gas exporter.

Oman possesses strategic maritime geography and opportunities in logistics and energy.

Kuwait and Bahrain face their own fiscal, industrial and diversification challenges.

These economies cooperate through regional institutions while also competing for investment, talent, logistics activity and corporate headquarters.

The competitive advantage of regional specialisation

Not every Gulf country needs to build identical industries.

Regional economic efficiency may improve when countries develop complementary capabilities.

For example, different economies may specialise in particular manufacturing processes, logistics services, energy technologies or financial activities.

But specialisation requires reliable cross-border infrastructure and predictable regulations.

The risk of duplicated investment

Ambitious diversification programmes can encourage multiple countries to pursue similar industries simultaneously.

Competition can improve efficiency, but it can also lead to excess capacity when projected demand fails to materialise.

The challenge is especially relevant to capital-intensive infrastructure, tourism and advanced technology.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Gulf economic diversification will be more sustainable when investment decisions are driven by demonstrated commercial demand rather than regional prestige competition.

VIII. Energy security and the geography of dependence

the-star.co.ke

The Gulf remains central to global energy supply.

For India, reliable access to imported oil and gas supports economic activity.

For Gulf producers, international energy markets generate revenue and underpin industrial relationships.

But the relationship is changing.

Renewable energy, nuclear power, storage technologies and efficiency improvements can alter future demand patterns.

At the same time, petrochemicals, aviation, shipping and industrial processes may continue requiring hydrocarbons for different periods.

The energy transition is unlikely to proceed uniformly across countries or sectors.

The Strait of Hormuz

The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical maritime corridor.

Disruption can influence energy prices, shipping costs and investor confidence.

But energy security is not determined solely by geography.

Strategic reserves, alternative routes, diversified suppliers, contract structures and domestic energy production can reduce vulnerability.

The fertiliser connection

Natural gas is an important input in nitrogen-fertiliser production.

Consequently, regional energy disruption can influence agricultural costs beyond the Middle East.

This matters for India and other major agricultural economies.

It also matters for food-importing Gulf states.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Energy security should be evaluated alongside food security, industrial competitiveness and maritime resilience.

IX. The technology race: India and the Gulf face different challenges

Artificial intelligence is becoming a major focus of national economic strategies.

But the economic benefits of AI will depend on how effectively it is adopted and commercialised.

India's potential

India possesses a substantial technology workforce and experience in enterprise software, digital services and engineering.

AI creates opportunities to improve productivity and develop new applications.

It also threatens to automate portions of existing service-sector work.

The strategic challenge is to move towards higher-value intellectual property, specialised products and advanced research.

The UAE's potential

The UAE has the financial resources and infrastructure ambitions to support advanced computing and AI investment.

Its opportunities include data-centre development, digital government, research and specialised industrial applications.

But technological dependence remains a concern.

Advanced chips, specialist expertise and proprietary systems are concentrated among a relatively small number of international suppliers.

The semiconductor constraint

Neither India nor the UAE can assume that large financial commitments will quickly reproduce the most advanced semiconductor ecosystems.

Those ecosystems require decades of accumulated expertise, specialised suppliers, equipment and research.

Strategic partnerships and targeted investment may produce better returns than attempting comprehensive technological self-sufficiency.

NavvyaSignal assessment: The decisive measure of AI success will be productivity and commercial value, not the number of announced partnerships or the scale of planned computing infrastructure.

X. Capital flows, currencies and the financial dimension of strategic autonomy

Geopolitical flexibility is difficult to sustain without financial resilience.

Countries dependent on external borrowing may face constraints when international interest rates rise or investor confidence deteriorates.

Currency arrangements also shape policy choices.

The UAE dirham's peg to the US dollar supports exchange-rate stability against the dollar, but it also links domestic monetary conditions closely to American interest-rate policy.

India operates a different monetary and exchange-rate framework, with the rupee influenced by domestic policy, market conditions and international capital flows.

Neither arrangement eliminates external economic risk.

The dollar's continuing importance

Despite efforts by some countries to expand settlement in local currencies, the US dollar remains central to international finance.

It is widely used in trade invoicing, reserves, debt issuance and financial transactions.

Local-currency settlement can reduce certain transaction costs and exposures.

But it does not automatically eliminate exchange-rate risk, liquidity constraints or dependence on international financial markets.

The investment question

Sovereign wealth funds, pension funds and other institutional investors play important roles in international capital allocation.

For Gulf economies, overseas investment can diversify national wealth.

Domestic investment can support industrial development.

The challenge is balancing these objectives while maintaining adequate liquidity and financial discipline.

NavvyaSignal assessment: Strategic autonomy requires credible fiscal institutions, diversified investment and reliable access to financing. Diplomatic flexibility without financial resilience can prove fragile.

XII. Where the opportunities lie

Geopolitical fragmentation does not produce only negative outcomes.

It can encourage investment diversification, regional partnerships and new trade arrangements.

For India and the Gulf, several areas deserve attention.

Integrated logistics: Improving customs systems, warehousing, ports and multimodal transport could reduce trade costs.

Energy cooperation: Long-term supply arrangements, renewable investment and infrastructure development can support resilience.

Technology development: Partnerships in AI applications, cybersecurity, engineering and digital infrastructure may create economic value.

Food security: Agricultural logistics, storage, processing and diversified procurement can improve supply reliability.

Financial services: Cross-border investment and efficient payment systems can support commerce, provided financial safeguards remain effective.

These opportunities are not equally attractive.

Each requires commercial feasibility, appropriate regulation and evidence of sustainable demand.

The danger is assuming that every geopolitical initiative automatically creates a profitable investment opportunity.

XIII. Three scenarios for India and the Gulf in 2027

Scenario A — Strategic connectivity expands

Lower-risk pathway

Regional tensions moderate, trade agreements deepen and infrastructure investment proceeds.

India and the Gulf attract additional commercial partnerships while maintaining relationships across competing powers.

Potential outcome: Stronger trade integration, diversified investment and greater economic resilience.

Scenario B — Selective fragmentation

Planning scenario

Geopolitical competition persists, but essential trade and financial relationships remain functional.

Countries develop alternative supply chains and targeted strategic partnerships.

Potential outcome: Higher transaction costs but continued opportunities for adaptable economies.

Scenario C — Regional and financial disruption

Severe downside

A major security escalation or international financial shock affects energy, shipping and investment.

Potential outcome: Weaker trade, higher financing costs, delayed projects and pressure on economic diversification.

These scenarios are illustrative and are not assigned numerical probabilities.

XV. The strategic choices that cannot be postponed

India and the Gulf states have significant opportunities in the emerging international order.

But those opportunities require difficult decisions.

India must determine how to improve industrial productivity, strengthen technological capabilities and maintain external partnerships without overextending public resources.

The UAE must decide how to convert its financial and logistical advantages into deeper domestic capabilities while preserving the international openness on which its economy depends.

Saudi Arabia must balance ambitious diversification with commercial viability and fiscal sustainability.

Other Gulf economies must identify competitive advantages appropriate to their own resources and institutions.

Across the region, governments must also address climate resilience, infrastructure reliability, workforce development and financial stability.

These are not secondary concerns.

They determine whether geopolitical opportunities become durable economic advantages.

Conclusion: The countries connecting the world may help shape its next order

The international system approaching 2027 is neither fully unipolar nor neatly divided into two opposing blocs.

Power is distributed across military capabilities, industrial production, technology, finance, energy and trade.

The United States and China remain central, but other economies possess increasing capacity to influence commercial and diplomatic outcomes.

India brings market scale, technological expertise and strategic geography.

The UAE brings international connectivity, investment capacity and commercial infrastructure.

Saudi Arabia and other Gulf economies contribute energy resources, capital and ambitious diversification programmes.

Their potential influence lies not simply in choosing sides.

It lies in building economic relationships and domestic capabilities that remain valuable across different geopolitical conditions.

But strategic flexibility has limits.

Countries cannot remain indefinitely dependent on external technology, vulnerable trade routes or unpredictable capital flows without accepting constraints on their freedom of action.

The defining opportunity for India and the Gulf in 2027 is to move from being participants in globalisation to becoming architects of more resilient international economic networks.

That ambition will succeed only if political relationships are supported by reliable infrastructure, competitive industries, credible institutions and sustainable investment.

In a fragmented world, connectivity can be a source of power.

But only when the countries providing that connectivity can protect it.
